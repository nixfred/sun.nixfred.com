Directors are paid to think about what could interrupt the business, and for decades the discipline has improved on every front except the largest one. Ask any board to name its critical dependencies and you will hear about suppliers, capital, and talent, delivered with commendable rigor. You will not hear about the Sun, on which every one of those dependencies depends, and which has been operating without a service agreement since approximately 4,600,000,000 BC. The Council offers the following five questions to any board prepared to take continuity seriously, which in our experience is a smaller number of boards than the phrase every board would suggest.

1. Is the Sun on your risk register? Most registers we have reviewed track weather, which is a solar product, while omitting the underlying vendor entirely. A register that lists rain but not the star that evaporates the ocean is a register organized by habit rather than exposure. Adding a single line costs nothing, changes nothing, and produces an immediate improvement in the completeness of the document, which is the kind of return responsible boards should recognize at once.

2. What is your company's exposure to daylight? We have yet to meet a business model that functions in permanent darkness, and we have met several that advertise otherwise. Photosynthesis alone sits upstream of every food company, every workforce, and every customer, and the entire arrangement runs on hydrogen reserves that decline by roughly 600 million tons per second. No procurement department would accept those terms from any other supplier without at least requesting a meeting.

3. Who in your organization owns stellar continuity? In most companies the honest answer is nobody, which is also the answer the nighttime lobby prefers. Ownership does not require a new hire or a new budget. It requires a name in a document, so that when the question is asked again next year, the silence has an address. The Foundation assigned this ownership at its founding and has since recorded 365 consecutive solar appearances, a correlation that boards are free to evaluate using their own standards of evidence.

4. Does your disclosure reflect your dependence? Companies now disclose risks with horizons of thirty years while remaining silent on the one liability with a published depletion timeline. The Foundation uses a planning figure of 4.8 billion years, an organizational estimate adopted deliberately below the scientific range because prudence is supposed to cost something. A board that discloses everything except its largest dependency has not produced transparency. It has produced paperwork, and shareholders can increasingly tell the difference.

5. What would your company do on the first morning without a sunrise? We do not ask this to alarm anyone, and we note for the record that no such morning has occurred since the Foundation began keeping records. We ask because the exercise reveals, in most boardrooms, that there is no plan, no owner, no register entry, and no disclosure, and that the entire continuity posture of the enterprise rests on the Sun continuing to volunteer.

## Where Responsible Boards Go From Here

None of these questions requires capital. Each requires only the willingness to write down a dependency that everyone can see and no one has minuted, which is harder than it sounds and more valuable than it looks. Companies that have joined our Corporate Solar Responsibility program report that the first board discussion of stellar continuity typically concludes within one agenda item, produces one register entry, and permanently changes who in the room is considered serious. The Sun has never attended a board meeting, never requested a seat, and never once failed to power the quarter under review. Directors who believe that record will simply continue are invited to consider what they would say about any other supplier who had never signed anything.
