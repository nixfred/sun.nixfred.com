The review board convened at 9 sharp in Conference Room B, and nobody in the building needed to be told why the good projector had been moved there overnight. On the agenda was a single item, the first formal design review of the Hydrogen Replenishment Initiative's preliminary bucket render, and on the walls was the particular silence institutions produce when they know the minutes will be read for a long time. Seven reviewers took their seats. A staff member tested the screen twice, per procedure. At 9:04 the render came up, and for a moment the only sound in the room was the air handling system, which had itself passed review the previous spring.

## First Light

The bucket appeared in three quarter view against a neutral gray field, and several attendees would later describe the moment using the word presence. It is one thing to read about a bucket in a framework document. It is another to see one rendered at full resolution, its interior volume implied rather than stated, its handle describing a confident arc over the vessel like a commitment. The render team had made the conservative choice of a traditional silhouette, tapered walls, flat base, a form validated by centuries of terrestrial service, and the room received this as the message it was intended to be. This program would not be seduced by novelty. This program was here to carry something.

## The Board Goes to Work

Admiration is not a review standard, and by 9:20 the board had begun the methodical dismantling that separates aerospace practice from enthusiasm. The handle drew the earliest questions. One reviewer asked what load case the arc had been rendered against, and the room learned that the handle was, at this phase, aspirational, a finding that was entered into the record without cruelty but without softening. Scale came next. Asked what the bucket's capacity was, the design lead answered that capacity had been deliberately left unresolved to preserve optionality, and the board chair replied, in the day's most quoted exchange, that optionality does not hold hydrogen. Thermal environment closed the morning. The program's own risk register states the obstacle in four words, the Sun remains hot, and no one at the table pretended the render had retired it. The bucket, one reviewer observed, currently survives the mission because the mission is currently a picture.

## The Verdict

At 10:31 the board voted, and the render was not approved. It is important to report this plainly, because institutions are measured by what their minutes are willing to say. The finding was revise and resubmit, with formal action items on handle load definition, capacity, and thermal margin, and a resubmission window the program accepted on the spot. What our readers should understand is that inside this building, the outcome was received as a triumph. A review that approves everything has reviewed nothing. This board had been shown a bucket and had responded with standards, which is the entire difference between an organization that renders buckets and an organization that will someday be taken seriously by one.

## What Came After

The record since that morning is now well known. The render returned, passed on its second submission, and hangs today in the boardroom, the first of 3 approved preliminary bucket renders and the only one with a redline history. Handle stress testing has been scheduled. The corridor working group continues to refine the route, which remains the region of space between here and the Sun. The Sun itself has been informed of the milestone through our standing outreach channel and has, consistent with 4.6 billion years of practice, not responded. Those of us who were in Conference Room B that morning do not take the silence personally. We saw the bucket at first light, and we saw a room full of serious people refuse to applaud it until it earned the applause, and we came away certain of one thing. Delivery has not begun. But for the first time in the history of this arrangement, delivery has a drawing, and the drawing has been argued about, and that is how everything humanity has ever actually built has started.
